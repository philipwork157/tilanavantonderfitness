<p class="article-intro"><span class="drop-cap">C</span><span>arbs.</span></p>

Say the word and suddenly we’re thinking about bread, pasta, pizza, burgers and all the delicious foods we’ve somehow been taught to feel slightly guilty about.

*“I shouldn’t eat that.”*

*“I’m trying to be good.”*

*“I’m cutting carbs this week.”*

But why are we so afraid of them?

Somewhere along the way, carbohydrates became the naughty child of the nutrition world. We’re told to cut them, avoid them, only eat them at certain times or somehow “earn” them through exercise.

Yet carbohydrates are one of our three main macronutrients and an incredibly useful source of fuel—particularly when you’re active.

Yes, carbs are delicious. Yes, some carbohydrate-rich foods can be very easy to overeat. And yes, lower-carbohydrate approaches can have a place for certain people and certain goals.

But if you’re trying to build a sustainable relationship with food, carbohydrates don’t necessarily need to disappear.

Perhaps carbs aren’t the problem.

Perhaps we just need to understand them better.

## Not all carbs come in a pizza box

When we hear “carbs,” most of us immediately picture bread.

But carbohydrates are found in far more foods than that.

Fruit contains carbohydrates. So do oats, rice, potatoes, beans, vegetables, grains, milk and yoghurt. And yes, bread, pasta, pizza and burgers can contain plenty of them too.

Carbohydrate and protein each provide approximately 4 calories per gram, while fat provides approximately 9 calories per gram.

One of carbohydrate’s important jobs is providing energy. Your body can break digestible carbohydrate down into glucose, which your cells can use for fuel. Carbohydrate can also be stored as glycogen, particularly in your muscles and liver.

Think of glycogen as fuel sitting in your tank.

If you’re running, lifting weights, cycling, walking or simply trying to survive a busy day, having some fuel in that tank can be rather useful.

## Simple carbs, complex carbs… what’s the difference?

You’ll often hear carbohydrates divided into “simple” and “complex” carbs.

Simple carbohydrates contain one or two sugar molecules and can generally be broken down relatively quickly. Complex carbohydrates are longer chains of sugar molecules and are found in foods such as grains, legumes and starchy vegetables.

But even that doesn’t tell the whole story.

The amount of fibre, how processed a food is and what you eat alongside it can all influence digestion and your blood-glucose response.

Fruit is a great example.

Fruit contains naturally occurring sugars such as fructose, but it also provides fibre, water, vitamins, minerals and other nutrients.

So a banana containing sugar doesn’t suddenly make bananas “bad.”

Nutrition needs context.

## Stop looking at the carb. Look at the meal.

This was something I had to learn.

Instead of staring suspiciously at a slice of bread, look at what you’re eating with it.

Sourdough with eggs and avocado.

Rice with chicken and vegetables.

Oats with yoghurt and berries.

Pasta with lean mince, vegetables and tomato sauce.

Suddenly you’re not just “eating carbs.” You’re eating a balanced meal containing carbohydrates, protein, fat, fibre and other nutrients.

Protein, fat and fibre can also contribute to fullness and influence how quickly a mixed meal is digested.

So sometimes the answer isn’t removing the carbohydrate.

It’s simply building a better meal around it.

Does that mean white bread is forbidden because wholegrain bread generally provides more fibre?

No.

Different foods simply have different nutritional qualities and uses.

And interestingly, sometimes faster-digesting carbohydrates can actually be useful.

Like when you’re training.

## I learnt this one the hard way…

Five or six years ago, I would happily go running or walk into the gym without eating.

Fuel?

Apparently optional.

Except I would become absolutely exhausted and then wonder why my body wasn’t giving me the performance I wanted.

Eventually, I started researching nutrition and experimenting with eating before I trained.

And it changed the way I looked at food.

I realised:

**I wasn’t exercising to earn my food. My food was helping me exercise.**

Before training, I personally enjoy something simple like sourdough or an English muffin with about 15 g peanut butter, honey and half a banana.

Nothing fancy.

No magical pre-workout meal.

Just fuel that works for me.

And your ideal pre-training food might look completely different. It depends on your body, the type of training you’re doing, how long you’re training for and what your stomach tolerates.

## Long runs are where carbs really became my friend

If you’ve ever been halfway through a long run and suddenly felt as though someone unplugged you from the wall, you’ll understand why fuelling matters.

For longer runs, I don’t like waiting until I’m exhausted before remembering that food exists.

I generally start taking in fuel around 30–40 minutes into my run, or roughly every 5–6 km depending on the session, and continue from there.

It might be a gel, banana or part of an energy bar, alongside fluids.

Sports-nutrition recommendations commonly suggest approximately 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour during prolonged endurance exercise, with higher amounts sometimes used during very long events.

That doesn’t mean you need to copy my exact timing or suddenly force down gels every 30 minutes.

Your training, duration, intensity and gut tolerance matter.

The point is much simpler:

**If you’re asking your body to perform for a long time, don’t wait until the fuel tank is completely empty before deciding to fill it.**

## Then you eat carbs and the scale betrays you…

You’ve been eating well.

You have a little more bread or pasta than usual.

You wake up the next morning.

Step onto the scale.

Excuse me?!

Before deciding that last night’s pasta somehow destroyed months of progress overnight, there’s something important to understand.

Your body stores carbohydrate as glycogen, and water is stored alongside that glycogen. Research commonly estimates roughly three grams of water—or sometimes more—alongside each gram of stored glycogen.

So if you’ve eaten more carbohydrates and replenished glycogen, your body weight may temporarily increase because you’re carrying more glycogen and associated water.

That isn’t the same thing as suddenly gaining that amount of body fat.

And this is exactly why I don’t like allowing one scale measurement to determine progress.

Your scale is actually quite… dumb.

Sorry, scale.

It doesn’t tell you:

“Don’t worry, that’s water.”

It doesn’t know you had more sodium yesterday.

It doesn’t know you’ve trained hard.

It doesn’t know what’s sitting in your digestive system.

It doesn’t know whether you’ve built muscle.

It just gives you a number and leaves you alone with your emotions.

Look at the trend over time, but also look beyond the scale.

Are you stronger?

Are your clothes fitting differently?

Are your measurements changing?

Are you running better?

Do you have more energy?

Are you building habits you can actually maintain?

Because body weight and body composition aren’t the same thing.

Muscle is denser than fat, and if you’re strength training and progressively overloading, your body can change even when the number on the scale doesn’t move dramatically.

Changing your body composition doesn’t happen overnight either.

It takes months.

Sometimes years.

I know this from my own journey.

Your body isn’t being shaped by one bowl of pasta. It’s being shaped by what you repeatedly do over time.

## And what about carb-loading?

Ah yes.

The traditional pre-race pasta party.

Carb-loading is actually a little more scientific than eating the biggest bowl of pasta you can find the night before a marathon.

For well-trained athletes preparing for endurance events such as a marathon, established sports-nutrition recommendations support approximately 10–12 g of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day during the final 36–48 hours before competition when the goal is to maximise glycogen stores.

Yes.

You read that correctly.

For a 70 kg runner, 10 g/kg equals 700 g of carbohydrate per day.

That’s a lot.

And that’s why this is a specific endurance-performance strategy—not a recommendation that everyone needs to eat that amount before their Saturday 5 km.

It’s also something you should practise rather than suddenly attempting for the first time before race day.

Personally, if I’m normally eating around 200 g of carbohydrates, I may simply start increasing my intake over the days leading into a race. My approach isn’t necessarily the same as full scientific carbohydrate loading, and what works for me won’t automatically be right for you.

And remember our conversation about glycogen and water?

If the scale goes up while you’re carb-loading, don’t panic.

You’re preparing for an endurance event.

The goal isn’t necessarily to arrive at the start line at your lightest.

The goal is to arrive fuelled.

## So… can I eat the burger?

Yes.

Does this mean every meal should now consist of burgers, pizza and pasta because Tilana said carbs are our friends?

Nice try.

That’s not the point either.

Nutrition doesn’t need another extreme.

Most of the time, prioritise nutritious carbohydrate sources that provide fibre, vitamins and minerals—fruit, vegetables, oats, potatoes, rice, whole grains and legumes.

But there can also be room for bread.

And pasta.

And pizza.

And the burger bun.

Because if your idea of “healthy eating” requires you to remove every food you genuinely enjoy, ask yourself how long you’re realistically going to maintain it.

Eight weeks?

Maybe.

Eight years?

Probably not.

Balance isn’t eating perfectly. Balance is understanding where everything fits.

Carbohydrates can fuel your training, replenish glycogen, provide important nutrients, make meals satisfying and help support an active lifestyle.

And sometimes…

They’re simply delicious.

Food is allowed to be that too.

So the next time you look at a piece of bread and wonder whether you “should” eat it, perhaps ask yourself a different question:

**What if I stopped being afraid of food and started learning how to use it?**

Learn what fuels your body. Learn what portions support your goals. Learn how different foods make you feel. Learn when you need more fuel and when you need less.

And most importantly, build a relationship with food that you could realistically maintain for years—not just until the next eight-week challenge ends.

One pizza won’t destroy your progress.

One salad won’t create it.

One higher number on the scale doesn’t mean you’ve failed.

Carbs aren’t the enemy.

Sometimes they’re exactly the fuel your body has been asking for.

And sometimes…

just keep the burger bun.
