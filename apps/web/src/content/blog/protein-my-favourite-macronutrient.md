<p class="article-intro"><span class="drop-cap">I</span><span>f carbs have a slightly complicated reputation, protein seems to have the opposite problem.</span></p>

Protein is everywhere in the fitness world.

Protein shakes. Protein bars. High-protein yoghurt. Protein pancakes. Chicken and rice. And, of course, the person at the gym carrying a shaker bottle large enough to hydrate a small family.

But protein genuinely deserves a lot of the attention it gets - especially if you’re strength training, trying to build or maintain muscle, or simply trying to create meals that leave you satisfied.

And I have to admit something before we go any further:

Protein is probably my favourite macronutrient because I just freaking LOVEEEE meat.

Give me a good steak and I’m happy.

But whether you love meat like I do, prefer eggs and dairy, or get most of your protein from plant foods, understanding why protein matters can completely change the way you build your meals.

## So, what does protein actually do?

Protein is made up of amino acids, which your body uses for far more than simply making your muscles bigger.

They’re involved in building and repairing tissues and making enzymes, hormones and other important compounds. Protein is essential throughout the body.

But for this blog, let’s talk about the part we fitness people love:

Muscle.

When you strength train, you’re giving your muscles a reason to adapt.

Training provides the stimulus.

Protein provides amino acids your body can use in the repair and rebuilding process.

Research shows that resistance exercise and protein intake work together to stimulate muscle protein synthesis - essentially the process involved in building and repairing muscle tissue. (PubMed)

This is why I always say:

You can’t just eat loads of protein and expect muscles to magically appear.

And you can’t train hard while completely ignoring your nutrition and expect optimal results either.

**Training and nutrition work together.**

## How much protein do you actually need?

This is where people often either underestimate their needs or go completely overboard.

For healthy people who exercise regularly, research supports approximately 1.4-2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day as sufficient for building and maintaining muscle for most exercising individuals. (PubMed)

So, for example:

If you weigh 60 kg, that range is approximately 84-120 g per day.

At 70 kg, it’s approximately 98-140 g.

At 80 kg, approximately 112-160 g.

That doesn’t mean everyone needs to immediately aim for the very top of the range.

Your training, goals, overall calorie intake, age and circumstances matter.

For someone strength training regularly and specifically trying to build or preserve muscle, somewhere within that evidence-based range can be a useful target.

And more isn’t automatically better.

You don’t win a prize for managing to put protein powder into every food you eat.

## Spread it out

Instead of reaching 8 p.m. and realising you’ve eaten approximately seven grams of protein all day...

...and then attempting to consume an entire chicken...

it can be much easier to distribute protein throughout your meals.

Sports-nutrition research commonly suggests around 20-40 g of high-quality protein per serving, or roughly 0.25 g/kg per serving as a general starting point, with protein spread across the day. (PubMed)

That might look like eggs and yoghurt at breakfast, chicken at lunch, yoghurt or another protein-rich snack later, and beef or fish with dinner.

Suddenly hitting your daily target becomes much easier.

And no, you don’t have to sprint towards a protein shake the second you finish your final squat.

Your total daily protein intake matters enormously. The muscle-building response to exercise also lasts well beyond the few minutes immediately after your workout. (PubMed)

## Do I need protein powder?

No.

Protein powder can be incredibly convenient, but it’s not magic.

If you’re able to get enough protein through your normal diet, wonderful.

Chicken, beef, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, soy foods and other protein-rich foods can all contribute.

If you’re busy and struggling to reach your target, a shake can simply make life easier.

That’s how I prefer looking at supplements in general:

**They’re there to supplement your diet - not become your diet.**

## Now let’s talk about meat

My favourite part.

Meat obviously provides high-quality protein, but there’s another interesting connection to strength training:

Creatine.

Creatine isn’t protein. It’s a naturally occurring compound that your body makes itself, and you also obtain some through food - particularly animal foods such as meat and fish. (Office of Dietary Supplements)

Your body stores most of its creatine in muscle, where the creatine/phosphocreatine system helps regenerate ATP - the energy currency your muscles use - particularly during short, high-intensity efforts.

Think:

Heavy lifting.

Sprinting.

Explosive movements.

Those last few difficult reps where you’re questioning all of your life decisions.

That’s one reason creatine is so popular in strength training.

Research shows that creatine supplementation can improve strength, power and performance during repeated short bursts of high-intensity activity, and over time it can support training adaptations. (Office of Dietary Supplements)

Your body naturally produces some creatine, and foods such as beef and salmon provide it too. The NIH notes roughly 2 g of creatine per pound of beef or salmon, although dietary intake provides much smaller amounts than the doses typically used in creatine supplementation. (Office of Dietary Supplements)

So eating steak isn’t the same thing as taking a standard creatine supplement.

But I will happily accept another reason to enjoy my steak.

## Protein isn’t just for bodybuilders

I think this is important.

You don’t need to be trying to look like a competitive bodybuilder before protein becomes relevant.

If you’re strength training, you want your body to recover.

If you’re running, you’re still putting stress through muscle tissue.

If you’re getting older, maintaining muscle becomes increasingly important.

And if you’re simply trying to build a strong, capable body, protein deserves a place on your plate.

For me, building muscle isn’t only about appearance.

I want to be strong.

I want to lift heavier.

I want my training to progress.

I want my body to be capable.

And I want that strength to follow me through the different seasons of my life.

That’s why strength training and nutrition go hand in hand.

## But protein isn’t the entire meal

For all the attention protein receives, it is important to remember this:

Protein isn’t supposed to replace every other macronutrient.

A beautiful steak is great.

Steak with potatoes, vegetables and a sauce you actually enjoy?

Even better.

Your carbohydrates can provide fuel for training. Protein provides the amino acids needed for repair and adaptation. Dietary fats have their own essential roles too.

We don’t need to keep making macronutrients fight each other.

**They are a team.**

Instead of making foods compete, learn what each macronutrient contributes and how they can work together.

Build meals that support your goals, help you feel strong and still allow you to enjoy your life.

You don’t need to eat perfectly.

You don’t need chicken and broccoli seven days a week.

And you definitely don’t need to live on protein shakes.

Start simply.

Look at your meals and ask:

**Where is my protein?**

Then build around it.

Over time, those little choices add up - especially when they’re combined with consistent strength training.

Because muscle isn’t built by one workout.

And it isn’t built by one steak either.

Unfortunately.

It’s built through repeatedly giving your body a reason to adapt, enough nutrition to support that adaptation, and enough time for the process to actually happen.

So eat your protein.

Lift your weights.

Be patient.

And if there’s steak involved...

I’ll happily join you.
